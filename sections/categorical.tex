\chapter{Composition and order}

This chapter makes the connection between composition, variable elimination,
and optimization precise. We develop the categorical and order-theoretic
structures that provide the foundation for the graphical language.
Readers familiar with symmetric monoidal categories and quantales may use
this chapter as a reference for our conventions.

\section{Relations and Quantales}

We begin with the most basic kind of relation:

\begin{definition}[Binary Relations]\label{def:binary-relations}
	Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets. A \emph{binary relation} $R$ from $X$ to $Y$ is a subset
	\[
		R \subseteq X \times Y.
	\]
	Equivalently, it can be identified with its characteristic function
	\[
		\chi_R : X \times Y \to \{0,+\infty\},
	\]
	defined by
	\[
		\chi_R(x,y) =
		\begin{cases}
			0  \text{ if } (x,y) \in R, \\
			+\infty \quad \text{otherwise}.
		\end{cases}
	\]
\end{definition}

A binary relation specifies whether two elements are related. We use two
representations: a subset of $X\times Y$ and its characteristic function,
which assigns $0$ to related pairs and $+\infty$ to unrelated pairs.
Although Boolean characteristic functions conventionally use truth values or
$\{0,1\}$, the $\{0,+\infty\}$ convention anticipates the cost-valued
relations introduced below.

The sets $X$ and $Y$ are arbitrary. Equipping them with additional structure
allows us to consider more specific classes of relations.
\begin{definition}[Linear/Affine Relations]
	Let $X$ and $Y$ be vector spaces. A \emph{linear relation} from $X$ to $Y$ is a linear subspace
	\[
		R \subseteq X \times Y.
	\]
	An \emph{affine relation} is an affine subspace of $X \times Y$.

	Equivalently, a linear relation can be described as the graph of a possibly multivalued linear operator, i.e.,
	\[
		R(x) := \{ y \in Y \mid (x,y) \in R \}.
	\]

	Alternatively, its characteristic function is defined by
	\[
		\chi_R : X \times Y \to \{0,+\infty\},
	\]

	\[
		\chi_R(x,y) =
		\begin{cases}
			0  \text{ if } (x,y) \in R, \\
			+\infty \quad \text{otherwise}.
		\end{cases}
	\]

\end{definition}

Thus, linear and affine relations are binary relations with additional
geometric structure on the set of related pairs.

Binary relations record only whether a pair is related. To attach a cost or
other numerical weight to each pair, we use the more general notion of
\emph{weighted relations} \cite{lawvere1973metric}.

\begin{definition}[Weighted Relations]
	Let $X$ and $Y$ be any two sets and let $\extendR^+:= [0, +\infty]$.
	A \emph{weighted relation} from $X$ to $Y$ is a set
	\[
		R \subseteq X \times Y \times \extendR^+
	\]
	such that for every $(x,y) \in X \times Y$ there exists \emph{exactly one}
	$r \in \extendR^+$ satisfying
	\[
		(x,y,r) \in R.
	\]

	Equivalently, we may define the characteristic function
	\[
		\chi_R : X \times Y \to \extendR^+
	\]
	by
	\[
		\chi_R(x,y) =
		r
		\quad \text{if and only if } (x,y,r) \in R.
	\]
\end{definition}

The codomain is not restricted to a binary set anymore. In fact, we could choose
any set of weights instead of $\extendR^+$. But so far we have only described
relations individually. We want more than that: we want to connect them, as we
did with the optimization problems in the previous chapter.

Let us first consider ordinary binary relations $R \subseteq X\times Y$ and
$S \subseteq Y\times Z$. If $x$ is related to $y$ by $R$, and $y$ is related to
$z$ by $S$, then we can connect the two relations through this shared $y$.
Their \emph{composition} is the relation
\[
S\circ R := \{(x,z)\in X\times Z \mid
\exists y\in Y:\ (x,y)\in R \text{ and } (y,z)\in S\}.
\]
Notice that $y$ is no longer part of the resulting pair. We only ask whether
some such $y$ exists. This is precisely the idea of eliminating an inner
variable while keeping the external ones.

Now, what happens when the relations carry weights? For each intermediate
choice of $y$, we also need to determine the weight of the connection.
If the weights represent costs, we add the costs of the two parts:
\[
\chi_R(x,y)+\chi_S(y,z).
\]
Different intermediate choices of $y$ may give different costs. As in the
optimization example of the previous chapter, we take the infimum over these
choices:
\[
\chi_{S\circ R}(x,z)
:=\inf_{y\in Y}\bigl(\chi_R(x,y)+\chi_S(y,z)\bigr).
\]
Here $+\infty$ represents an unavailable connection, and we use
$\inf\varnothing=+\infty$. If the weights are only $0$ and $+\infty$,
this formula gives $0$ exactly when some intermediate choice satisfies both
relations. Thus, it recovers ordinary relational composition.

So, choosing a set of weights is not enough to compose weighted relations,
we also need an operation to combine weights along a connection and another
to aggregate the possible intermediate choices.
For costs, these are addition and infimum; other kinds
of weights may call for other operations.

Furthermore, connecting three relations should give the same result regardless
of how we group the connections, and inserting an identity relation should
change nothing. To support this, we ask for an associative combination with
a unit, arbitrary aggregation, and distributivity of combination over
aggregation. These requirements lead us to another mathematical structure:
a \emph{quantale}.

\begin{definition}[Quantale]
	A \emph{quantale} is a structure $(Q,\leq,\otimes,e)$ such that:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item $(Q,\leq)$ is a complete lattice, i.e., every subset $A \subseteq Q$ admits a join $\bigvee A$ and a meet $\bigwedge A$;
		\item $(Q,\otimes,e)$ is a monoid;
		\item Multiplication distributes over arbitrary joins in each variable:
		      \[
			      a \otimes \Big( \bigvee_{i \in I} b_i \Big)
			      =
			      \bigvee_{i \in I} (a \otimes b_i),
			      \qquad
			      \Big( \bigvee_{i \in I} a_i \Big) \otimes b
			      =
			      \bigvee_{i \in I} (a_i \otimes b)
		      \]
		      for all families $\{a_i\}_{i\in I}, \{b_i\}_{i\in I} \subseteq Q$.
	\end{itemize}

	If $\otimes$ is commutative, the quantale is called \emph{commutative}.
	If $e = \top$ (the top element of the lattice), it is called \emph{integral}.
\end{definition}

Each of these three axioms earns its place. The complete lattice gives
meaning to the join $\bigvee$, which aggregates the possible intermediate
choices. The monoid $(Q,\otimes,e)$ combines weights along a connection,
with $e$ the weight of doing nothing. Distributivity lets this combination
pass through the aggregation, so that composing three relations gives the
same result regardless of how we group them.

Notice that the definition uses joins, while our cost example used an infimum.
For $\extendR^+=[0,+\infty]$, we choose the reversed numerical order:
$a\leq_Q b$ means $a\geq b$ numerically. The join in this order is precisely
the numerical infimum. With addition as $\otimes$ and $0$ as the unit,
we recover the operations used above. Thus, a quantale structures the weights
in a way that supports composition; it is not itself a relation.

Given a quantale $Q$, we can define the notion of $Q$-valued relation \cite{coecke2018generalizedrelations}.

\begin{definition}[Generalized Relations]
	A \emph{generalized relation} (or $Q$-valued relation) from $X$ to $Y$, where $Q$ is a quantale, is a set
	\[
		R \subseteq X \times Y \times Q
	\]
	such that for every $(x,y) \in X \times Y$ there exists \emph{exactly one}
	$q \in Q$ satisfying
	\[
		(x,y,q) \in R.
	\]

	Its characteristic function is
	\[
		\chi_R: X \times Y \to Q
	\]
	defined by
	\[
		\chi_R(x,y) = q
		\quad \text{if and only if } (x,y,q) \in R.
	\]
\end{definition}

A $Q$-valued relation, or a $Q$-relation, is, in essence, a way to
assign weights to pairs of elements. The quantale structure allows
us to compose these relations: for each intermediate $y$, we combine
the weights of the two connections, and then aggregate all possible
intermediate choices. Thus, $ (H \circ R)(x,z) = \bigvee_{y \in Y} R(x,y) \otimes H(y,z)$.
For our cost quantale, this means adding the costs along each
connection and taking the infimum over the intermediate choices:
$ (H \circ R)(x,z) = \inf_{y \in Y}\bigl(R(x,y)+H(y,z)\bigr)$.

One can recall our initial examples using this new structure:

\begin{example}

	The notion of a $Q$-valued relation recovers familiar concepts for particular choices of quantale.

	\medskip

	\noindent
	\textbf{(1) Truth quantale.}
	Let $Q = \{0,1\}$ with the usual order $0 \leq 1$, join given by $\lor$,
	and monoidal product $\otimes = \land$ with unit $e = 1$.
	This is the (Boolean) truth quantale.

	A $Q$-valued relation is then a function
	\[
		R : X \times Y \to \{0,1\}.
	\]
	Such a function is precisely the characteristic function of an ordinary
	binary relation
	\[
		\widetilde{R} \subseteq X \times Y,
		\qquad
		(x,y) \in \widetilde{R}
		\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
		R(x,y) = 1.
	\]
	Thus $Q$-valued relations coincide with classical binary relations.

	This also settles the indicator convention adopted in
	Definition~\ref{def:binary-relations}, where relations were valued in
	$\{0,+\infty\}$ rather than $\{0,1\}$. The two are the same quantale: the map
	$1 \mapsto 0$, $0 \mapsto +\infty$ sends $\lor$ to $\min$, $\land$ to $+$,
	and the unit $1$ to the unit $0$, hence is an isomorphism of the truth
	quantale onto the sub-quantale $\{0,+\infty\}$ of the Lawvere quantale below.
	We work with $\{0,+\infty\}$ throughout, so that Boolean relations sit inside
	the cost-valued ones without any change of convention.

	\medskip

	\noindent
	\textbf{(2) Lawvere quantale.}
	Let $Q = \extendR^+ = [0, +\infty]$ ordered by the
	\emph{reverse} of the usual order (so that $a \leq b$ iff $a \geq b$
	in the usual order), with monoidal product given by addition
	\[
		a \otimes b := a + b,
	\]
	and unit $e = 0$. Joins are then given by infima in the usual order.

	This structure is the Lawvere quantale.
	A $Q$-valued relation
	\[
		R : X \times Y \to \extendR^+
	\]
	is precisely a weighted relation assigning a (possibly infinite) cost
	to each pair $(x,y)$.


\end{example}
Hence generalized relations unify ordinary binary relations and
weighted relations as special cases corresponding to particular quantales.

Note that linear and affine relations are not recovered by this construction,
since they demand extra structure
on the sets $X$ and $Y$, specifically that $R$ be a linear or affine subspace of
$X \times Y$. They are, nonetheless,
fully embedded in the framework of binary relations.

To allow negative costs and unbounded objective values, we extend the Lawvere
quantale to the entire extended real line:

\begin{definition}[Optimization/Extended Lawvere Quantale]
	Let
	\[
		\overline{\mathbb{R}} := \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty,+\infty\}.
	\]
	The \emph{optimization quantale} is the structure
	\[
		(\overline{\mathbb{R}},\geq,\otimes,e)
	\]
	defined as follows:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item The order $\geq$ is the usual order on the extended real line.
		      With this order, arbitrary joins are given by the usual infima:
		      \[
			      \bigvee A = \inf A.
		      \]

		\item The monoidal product is addition:
		      \[
			      a \otimes b := a + b,
		      \]
		      where addition is extended to $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$ by
		      $a + (+\infty) = +\infty$ for every $a$, and
		      $a + (-\infty) = -\infty$ for every $a \neq +\infty$.
		      In particular we adopt the convention
		      \[
			      (+\infty) + (-\infty) := +\infty,
		      \]
		      so that $+\infty$ is absorbing. This is Moreau's
		      \emph{inf-addition} \cite{rockafellar1970convex}, and it is the
		      only extension under which the structure below is a quantale.

		\item The unit element is
		      \[
			      e := 0.
		      \]
	\end{itemize}

	With these operations,
	$(\overline{\mathbb{R}},\geq,+,0)$
	is a commutative quantale. Joins are ordinary infima,
	and addition distributes over arbitrary joins.
\end{definition}

The convention above is not a matter of taste. Without it, $+$ is only
partially defined on $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$, so
$(\overline{\mathbb{R}},+,0)$ fails to be a monoid and the quantale axioms
cannot even be stated. Following the pattern, 
we can build a $Q$-relation out of the previous quantale.

\begin{definition}[Optimization (Cost/Reward) Relations]
	Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets.
	An \emph{optimization relation} from $X$ to $Y$ is an
	$\overline{\mathbb{R}}$-valued relation, i.e., a function
	\[
		R : X \times Y \to \overline{\mathbb{R}}.
	\]

	Equivalently, it is a subset
	\[
		R \subseteq X \times Y \times \overline{\mathbb{R}}
	\]
	such that for every $(x,y) \in X \times Y$ there exists exactly one
	$r \in \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ satisfying
	\[
		(x,y,r) \in R.
	\]

	The value $R(x,y)$ is interpreted as the objective value
	(cost or reward) associated to the pair $(x,y)$,
	with $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ representing extremal infeasibility
	or unboundedness.
\end{definition}

Optimization relations extend nonnegative weighted relations to negative
values. The values $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ allow us to represent infeasibility
and unboundedness below, respectively.

Quantale-valued relations can also be viewed as matrices. Under this
identification, composition is matrix multiplication with joins and quantale
multiplication in place of ordinary addition and multiplication.

\begin{remark}
	A $Q$-valued relation $R : X \times Y \to Q$ is nothing but a
	\emph{$Q$-valued matrix} indexed by $X \times Y$: one simply reads
	off the entry at position $(x,y)$ as $\chi_R(x,y) \in Q$.
	That is, a generalized relation is precisely an element of $Q^{X \times Y}$.

\end{remark}

	\medskip
	\noindent
	When $X$ and $Y$ are finite sets, say $|X| = m$ and $|Y| = n$,
	this becomes completely explicit: a $Q$-valued relation is exactly
	an $m \times n$ matrix
	\[
		M_R \;=\; \bigl[\chi_R(x_i,\, y_j)\bigr]_{\substack{1 \leq i \leq m \\ 1 \leq j \leq n}}
		\;\in\; Q^{m \times n},
	\]
	whose $(i,j)$-entry records the $Q$-value assigned to the pair
	$(x_i, y_j)$.

	\medskip
	\noindent
	Moreover, composition of $Q$-valued relations corresponds precisely
	to \emph{matrix multiplication in $Q$}.
	Given a second $Q$-valued relation $S : Y \times Z \to Q$, their
	composite $S \circ R : X \times Z \to Q$ is defined by
	\[
		\chi_{S \circ R}(x, z)
		\;=\;
		\bigvee_{y \in Y}\, \chi_R(x,y) \otimes \chi_S(y,z),
	\]
	which, in matrix notation, reads
	\[
		M_{S \circ R} \;=\; M_R \otimes M_S,
		\qquad
		(M_R \otimes M_S)_{ij}
		\;=\;
		\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}\, (M_R)_{ik} \otimes (M_S)_{kj}.
	\]

	This is the usual row-times-column pattern, with ordinary addition replaced
by the join $\bigvee$ and ordinary multiplication replaced by $\otimes$.
Associativity of $\otimes$ and its distributivity over arbitrary joins make
this matrix composition associative.

For cost-valued relations, the same construction adds costs along a connection
and takes the infimum over intermediate choices. This is the composition used
for linear programs in the preceding chapter.


\section{Categories and semantics}

These constructions expose the order-theoretic structure of relational
composition: weights are combined along each intermediate connection and then
aggregated using a join~\cite[Section~2.5.3]{fong2019seven}. For costs, this join
is an infimum. The resulting value need not be attained by any intermediate
choice.

Linear programs and cost-valued relations therefore share a compositional
operation. Category theory provides a framework for making this connection
precise. As John Baez writes~\cite[Section~2, p.~6]{baez2004quantum},
\enquote{every sufficiently good analogy is yearning to become a functor}.

Readers unfamiliar with categories and functors may consult
Appendix~\ref{appendix:category-theory}, particularly
Section~\ref{appendix:category-basics}.

We first construct a category of $Q$-valued relations for any quantale $Q$.
To describe independent systems in parallel, we also need a tensor product
that combines their interfaces and morphisms.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric monoidal category]\label{def:main-smc}
A \emph{symmetric monoidal category} is a category $\mathcal C$ with a tensor
product $\otimes$, a unit object $I$, and coherent natural isomorphisms for
associativity, units, and symmetry. For $f:A\to B$ and $g:C\to D$, the tensor gives
\[
 f\otimes g:A\otimes C\longrightarrow B\otimes D.
\]
It preserves identities and satisfies the interchange law:
\[
 (f_2\circ f_1)\otimes(g_2\circ g_1)
 =
 (f_2\otimes g_2)\circ(f_1\otimes g_1).
\]
The symmetry $\sigma_{A,B}:A\otimes B\to B\otimes A$ exchanges the two
interfaces. The unit $I$ represents an empty interface.
\end{definition}

In other words, composing independent systems separately or first placing them
in parallel gives the same result. We suppress the canonical associativity and
unit isomorphisms in our notation; this does not mean they are identities in
every category. The coherence conventions are discussed in
Appendix~\ref{appendix:smc}.

The following proposition constructs the category of $Q$-valued relations.
When $Q$ is commutative, this category is symmetric monoidal and compact closed.
Compact closure is defined in Appendix~\ref{appendix:compact-closed}.
The construction is described in~\cite{coecke2018generalizedrelations}:

\begin{proposition}[Category $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$]
	For a quantale $Q$, the $Q$-relations form a category \textbf{Rel}(Q) with composition and identities

	\[
		(S\circ R) (a,c) = \bigvee_b R(a,b) \otimes S(b,c) \quad 1_A(a,b) = \bigvee \{k | a=b\}
	\]

	If $Q$ is a commutative quantale, then \textbf{Rel}(Q) carries a symmetric monoidal structure, with the categorical monoidal product $\bigotimes_{\mathcal{C}}$ on objects given by the Cartesian product of sets and the action on relations given for $R:A \to C$ and $S:B\to D$ by

	\[
		(R\otimes_{\mathcal{C}} S)(a,b,c,d) = R(a,c) \otimes S(b,d)
	\]

	The singleton set is the monoidal unit, and the category is compact closed with respect to this monoidal structure.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
	We verify the categorical structure.

	\medskip
	\noindent
	\textbf{(1) Well-defined composition.}
	Let $R:A \to B$ and $S:B \to C$ be $Q$-relations.
	For each $(a,c) \in A \times C$, the set
	\[
		\{ R(a,b)\otimes S(b,c) \mid b \in B \}
		\subseteq Q
	\]
	admits a supremum since $Q$ is a complete lattice.
	Hence $(S\circ R)(a,c)$ is well-defined as an element of $Q$.

	\medskip
	\noindent
	\textbf{(2) Associativity.}
	Let $R:A\to B$, $S:B\to C$, and $T:C\to D$.
	For $a\in A$, $d\in D$,
	\[
		((T\circ S)\circ R)(a,d)
		=
		\bigvee_{c}
		\Big(
		\bigvee_{b} R(a,b)\otimes S(b,c)
		\Big)
		\otimes T(c,d).
	\]
	Since $\otimes$ distributes over arbitrary suprema,
	\[
		=
		\bigvee_{b,c}
		R(a,b)\otimes S(b,c)\otimes T(c,d).
	\]
	Similarly,
	\[
		(T\circ (S\circ R))(a,d)
		=
		\bigvee_{b}
		R(a,b)\otimes
		\Big(
		\bigvee_{c} S(b,c)\otimes T(c,d)
		\Big)
		=
		\bigvee_{b,c}
		R(a,b)\otimes S(b,c)\otimes T(c,d).
	\]
	Thus composition is associative.

	\medskip
	\noindent
	\textbf{(3) Identities.}
	For each set $A$, define
	\[
		1_A(a,b)
		=
		\begin{cases}
			e    & \text{if } a=b,   \\
			\bot & \text{otherwise},
		\end{cases}
	\]
	where $\bot = \bigvee \varnothing$ is the bottom element of $Q$.

	Then for $R:A\to B$,
	\[
		(R\circ 1_A)(a,b)
		=
		\bigvee_{a'}
		1_A(a,a')\otimes R(a',b).
	\]
	All terms vanish except when $a'=a$, so
	\[
		=
		e \otimes R(a,b)
		=
		R(a,b).
	\]
	Similarly, $1_B\circ R = R$. Hence identities exist.

	\medskip
	\noindent
	\textbf{(4) Monoidal structure (commutative case).}
	Assume $Q$ is commutative.
	Define the tensor on objects by Cartesian product:
	\[
		A\otimes_{\mathcal{C}} B := A\times B.
	\]
	For relations $R:A\to C$ and $S:B\to D$, define
	\[
		(R\otimes_{\mathcal{C}} S)((a,b),(c,d))
		=
		R(a,c)\otimes S(b,d).
	\]
	Functoriality follows from associativity and commutativity of $\otimes$:
	\[
		(S_1\otimes S_2)\circ (R_1\otimes R_2)
		=
		(S_1\circ R_1)\otimes (S_2\circ R_2).
	\]
	The singleton set $1$ is a unit since $A\times 1 \cong A$.

	Symmetry is induced by the swap map
	\[
		\sigma_{A,B}((a,b),(b',a'))=
		\begin{cases}
			e    & \text{if } a=a',\, b=b', \\
			\bot & \text{otherwise}.
		\end{cases}
	\]

	\medskip
	\noindent
	\textbf{(5) Compact closure.}
	For each set $A$, define unit and counit relations
	\[
		\eta : 1 \to A\times A,
		\qquad
		\epsilon : A\times A \to 1
	\]
	by
	\[
		\eta(*,(a,a')) =
		\begin{cases}
			e    & a=a',             \\
			\bot & \text{otherwise},
		\end{cases}
		\qquad
		\epsilon((a,a'),*) =
		\begin{cases}
			e    & a=a',             \\
			\bot & \text{otherwise}.
		\end{cases}
	\]
	A direct computation using the definition of composition shows that the
	triangle identities hold:
	\[
		(1_A\otimes \epsilon)\circ (\eta\otimes 1_A) = 1_A,
		\qquad
		(\epsilon\otimes 1_A)\circ (1_A\otimes \eta) = 1_A.
	\]
	Thus every object is self-dual, and $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$ is compact closed.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Category of weighted relations]\label{example:weighted-relations}
	Let
	\[
		Q = ([0,+\infty], \le_Q, \otimes, e)
	\]
	be the nonnegative Lawvere quantale~\cite{lawvere1973metric,fong2019seven} where:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $a \le_Q b \iff a \ge b$ in the usual order;
		\item $\otimes = +$;
		\item $e = 0$;
		\item joins are given by infima in the usual order.
	\end{itemize}

	A $Q$-valued relation $R : A \to B$ is a function
	\[
		R : A \times B \to [0,+\infty],
	\]
	assigning a cost (or weight) to each pair $(a,b)$.

	The composition formula becomes
	\[
		(S \circ R)(a,c)
		=
		\inf_{b \in B}
		\big(
		R(a,b) + S(b,c)
		\big).
	\]

    Thus composition is given by \emph{infimal convolution}~\cite{stein2024compositional}. 

	Since addition is commutative, the Lawvere quantale is commutative,
	and $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$ carries a symmetric monoidal structure:

	\begin{itemize}
		\item On objects:
		      \[
			      A \otimes B := A \times B.
		      \]

		\item On morphisms:
		      \[
			      (R \otimes S)((a,b),(c,d))
			      =
			      R(a,c) + S(b,d).
		      \]
	\end{itemize}

	The singleton set is the monoidal unit.
	With this tensor product, $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$ is a symmetric monoidal
	compact closed category of weighted relations.

	In particular:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item If $A,B,C$ are finite sets, relations may be viewed as matrices with entries in $[0,+\infty]$.
		\item Composition is matrix multiplication in the $(\min,+)$-semiring.
	\end{itemize}

	Hence $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$ is a category of nonnegative weighted relations, which underlies dynamic programming, tropical algebra, and shortest-path problems.
	For costs that also admit negative values and $-\infty$, we instead use the
	optimization quantale defined above, with its inf-addition convention.
\end{example}

This example illustrates how the quantale structure supports 
categorical constructions: composition of $Q$-valued relations 
corresponds to matrix multiplication driven by $\bigvee$,
and the tensor product of relations is governed by the multiplication $\otimes$,
both operations coming directly from the quantale axioms.

These constructions provide exactly the semantic foundation for the algebra we aim to build.
In the same way that semantics is understood in linguistics and programming language
theory \cite{winskel1993formal}, the semantics of a formal system assigns meaning 
to its expressions. More precisely, while syntax consists of the tokens and rules for 
forming and combining expressions, semantics is the interpretation assigned to each
such expression — in our case, a cost-valued relation living in $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$.

As outlined in the introduction, the central goal of this work is to show that
optimization is merely a particular case of a more general algebraic and categorical structure.
The key mechanism enabling this is the infimal composition present in the definition
of the Costs/Rewards category: composing two relations automatically performs variable
elimination, which is precisely the fundamental operation underlying optimization.

To make this precise,
we need both a semantics — provided by the categorical framework developed above —
and a syntax: a rigorous, compositional language for expressing optimization problems
symbolically. The map between those is a structure-preserving map, a symmetric monoidal
functor, from the syntactic layer into the semantic one.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric monoidal functor]\label{def:main-monoidal-functor}
A \emph{strong symmetric monoidal functor} $F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D$ is a
functor with coherent natural isomorphisms
\[
 FA\otimes FB\longrightarrow F(A\otimes B),
 \qquad I_{\mathcal D}\longrightarrow F(I_{\mathcal C}),
\]
compatible with associativity, units, and symmetry. When these comparisons
are identities, the functor is called \emph{strict}.
\end{definition}

For our interpretations, this preservation is written as
\[
 \llbracket g\circ f\rrbracket
 =\llbracket g\rrbracket\circ\llbracket f\rrbracket,
 \qquad
 \llbracket f\otimes g\rrbracket
 =\llbracket f\rrbracket\otimes\llbracket g\rrbracket.
\]
where $\llbracket \rrbracket$ is our interpretation map.
This map takes a symbol or expression in our syntax and maps it to its meaning.
For the purpose here, the interpretation map will take diagrams
(the symbols of our syntax) and map them to LP value functions, represented as
cost-valued relations. 
Identities, the unit, and symmetry are preserved as well. Thus, interpreting
a compound diagram gives the same result as interpreting its components and
then combining their meanings. For a general strong functor, the corresponding
identifications use its comparison isomorphisms rather than literal equalities.
The formal compatibility conditions are recalled in
Appendix~\ref{appendix:monoidal-functor} and the definition of the map is presented
later in this section.


The graphical calculi used in this thesis have one generating wire type.  An
interface is therefore determined only by its number of wires.

\begin{definition}[PROP]\label{def:main-prop}
A \emph{PROP} is a strict symmetric monoidal category whose objects are the
natural numbers, whose tensor product on objects is addition, and whose
monoidal unit is $0$.  A morphism $m\to n$ is drawn with $m$ input wires and
$n$ output wires.
\end{definition}

Thus $1$ is the basic wire, $n=1^{\otimes n}$ is a bundle of $n$ wires, and
$m\otimes n=m+n$.  Matrices provide the standard example: for a field
$\mathbb K$, let $\mathbf{Mat}_{\mathbb K}$ have morphisms $m\to n$ given by
$n\times m$ matrices.  Composition is matrix multiplication, tensor is block
diagonal sum, and the symmetry is a permutation matrix.

A morphism of PROPs is a strict symmetric monoidal functor that is the
identity on objects. It preserves the number of inputs and outputs, sequential
composition, parallel composition, identities, and symmetries
\cite[Section~2.1]{bonchi2017interacting}.

The syntactic layer will be formalized as a Symmetric Monoidal Theory (SMT)
\cite{bonchi2017interacting}, which is a presentation of a category by generators
and equations, whose terms respect the compositional structure of a symmetric monoidal
category. This is the standard tool for building graphical languages, since it provides
the necessary structures to allow for parallel and sequential composition.

\begin{definition}[Symmetric Monoidal Theory]
	An SMT is a pair $(\Sigma,E)$, where $\Sigma$ is a signature of operators,
	each with a specified arity $m$ and coarity $n$, and $E$ is a set of equations.

	\medskip

	$\Sigma$-terms are defined inductively as the smallest collection of
	typed expressions $t : m \to n$ such that:

	\begin{enumerate}

		\item (Generators) If $o \in \Sigma$ with $o : m \to n$, then $o$ is a $\Sigma$-term of type $m \to n$.
		\item (Identity)
		      For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$, there is a term
		      \[
			      \mathrm{id}_n : n \to n.
		      \]

		\item (Composition)
		      If
		      \[
			      a : m \to n
			      \quad \text{and} \quad
			      b : n \to k
		      \]
		      are $\Sigma$-terms, then
		      \[
			      a ; b : m \to k
		      \]
		      is a $\Sigma$-term.

		\item (Tensor product)
		      If
		      \[
			      a : m_1 \to n_1
			      \quad \text{and} \quad
			      b : m_2 \to n_2
		      \]
		      are $\Sigma$-terms, then
		      \[
			      a \otimes b : m_1 + m_2 \to n_1 + n_2
		      \]
		      is a $\Sigma$-term.

		\item (Symmetry)   For all $m,n \in \mathbb{N}$, there is a term
		      \[
			      \sigma_{m,n} : m+n \to n+m.
		      \]
	\end{enumerate}

	\medskip

	The equations in $E$ relate $\Sigma$-terms of the same type.
\end{definition}

We represent generators $o:m\to n$ in $\Sigma$ as string diagrams.
The $\Sigma$-terms are built inductively by sequential and parallel composition
of these generators and the structural terms. The set $E$ supplies the
axiomatic equations between diagrams.

The categorical counterpart of these terms is the free category generated by the SMT.

\begin{definition}[The Free category of SMT]
	The category $\text{FreeP}_{(\Sigma,E)}$ is freely generated by $(\Sigma,E)$ where the objects are natural numbers, morphisms are $\Sigma$-terms modulo the symmetric monoidal structural laws and the smallest congruence generated by $E$. This congruence is closed under composition and tensor product, so these operations are well-defined on equivalence classes. $\text{FreeP}_{(\Sigma,E)}$ is in fact a PROP\footnote{For the definition of a PROP, see Definition~\ref{def:main-prop};
its presentation by an SMT is recalled in Appendix~\ref{appendix:graphical-theories}.}
\end{definition}

The $\text{FreeP}_{(\Sigma,E)}$, also denoted as $P\Sigma_{/E}$,
\footnote{Throughout this work, we use $\text{FreeP}_{S}$ and $S$, for
	whatever syntax $S$ may be, interchangeably.}
will be our \textbf{syntax} responsible for expressing our underlying semantics.

An SMT supports equational reasoning: proofs apply equations in $E$ within
larger terms, together with the symmetric monoidal structural laws.
Sequential and parallel composition provide the contexts in which these
local substitutions are made.

This resembles formal reasoning about computer programs, which may also be
composed sequentially or in parallel
\cite{winskel1993formal,piedeleu2023introductionstringdiagramscomputer}.
Here, the components are linear programs, and the aim is to reason about their
compositions using equations in a domain-specific language. To interpret them,
we need a map from those symbols into objects with more expressive meaning.
 
 
\begin{definition}[Interpretation]\label{def:glp-interpretation}
    The interpretation
    $\llbracket{-}\rrbracket:\mathrm{FreeP}_{\mathbf{P\Sigma_{/E}}}\to\mathcal{C}$
    is the strict symmetric monoidal functor that sends each
    generator of $\mathbf{P\Sigma}$ to its semmantics, read as a
    object in the category $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

$\mathcal{C}$ here is read as the semantics, that is, the objects and structures
in which we are interested. A syntax is then another category whose exists a 
functor $\llbracket \rrbracket$ which maps their objects in the semantics in a suitable
manner. One can argue to which extent one is really \textit{semantics} and
the other \textit{syntax}.

We often consider the \textit{syntax} the objects we are interested in using to
write down the proofs and our reasoning,
we can then impose over it suitable properties, such as those from SMTs, making
their use very convenient. In the other hand, semantics are often the objects
onto one wants to understand facts and propositions about it.

One could say that the semantics
objects are "more close related" to the actual objects in which it thinks about.
However, the truth is that when one writes $\llbracket z \rrbracket = f(x,y)$ it is just
mapping symbols into symbols and the real meaning comes from the very act of reading it.
We abstain from such philosophical dispute, here those terms
are differentiated by the way we intent to use them and we will denote as \textit{semantics}
the traditional mathematical representation of the objects of interest.

   
To connect these proofs and equations to the intended semantics, we require soundness,
completeness, and expressivity. These properties specify which semantic
statements the calculus can establish and which relations it can represent.

\begin{definition}
	We say that an SMT axiomatizes or represents a specific semantic PROP when its interpretation is sound, complete, and expressive. Soundness gives the commuting diagram below; completeness and expressivity give the further properties described afterward.
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\input{tikz/semDiag.tex}
	\end{figure}
	Here $P\Sigma$ is the free PROP of $\Sigma$-terms modulo the symmetric monoidal structural laws; $P\Sigma_{/E} = \text{FreeP}_{(\Sigma,E)}$ is its quotient by the congruence generated by $E$; and $q : P\Sigma \to P\Sigma_{/E}$ witnesses this quotient. $\mathbf{Sem}$ is our intended semantic PROP, with natural numbers indexing the chosen interfaces, such as $\mathbb{R}^n$, rather than all the objects of $\mathbf{Rel}(Q)$. The map $s$ is the interpretation of raw terms restricted to its semantic image; it need not be faithful. The arrow $\bar{s}$ exists when this interpretation is independent of the representative of an equivalence class. We distinguish three properties:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Soundness: We say that the interpretation is sound if $c \overset{E}= d$\footnote{$\overset{E}=$ means that this equality is provable using $E$ and the symmetric monoidal structural laws.} implies $\llbracket c\rrbracket = \llbracket d\rrbracket$. This is exactly what makes the induced interpretation on $P\Sigma_{/E}$ well-defined.
		\item Completeness: We say that the interpretation is complete if $\llbracket c\rrbracket = \llbracket d\rrbracket$ implies $c \overset{E}= d$. Once soundness holds, the induced interpretation is then faithful.
		\item Expressivity: We say that the interpretation is expressive if for every morphism $f:n\to m$ in $\mathbf{Sem}$ there exists a term $d:n\to m$ such that $\llbracket d\rrbracket = f$. Once soundness holds, the induced interpretation into $\mathbf{Sem}$ is then full.
	\end{itemize}
	Soundness and completeness make $\bar{s}$ an isomorphism onto the semantic image. Expressivity makes that image the whole intended semantic PROP. These statements concern the chosen interfaces and their hom-sets~\cite[Section~2.1]{bonchi2017interacting}; see also Appendix~\ref{appendix:graphical-theories}.
\end{definition}

Those three properties capture the idea that everything we can prove in the syntax holds in the semantics (soundness), every equality that holds in the semantics is provable in the syntax (completeness) and every morphism in the semantics has a syntactic representative (expressivity). Moreover, these properties describe the interpretation map both categorically and functionally.
